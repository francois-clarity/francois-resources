What do you do when you need something?

Not in a fight. On an ordinary Tuesday, when something has gone wrong in your day and your partner is in the next room.

Maybe you walk in and say it straight out. Maybe you go and stand in the kitchen near your partner, doing nothing in particular, until you get asked. Maybe you say nothing at all, sort it out yourself, and she hears about it on Friday once it is over.

Whichever one you do, you learned it long before you met her. She learned a different one. Neither of you has ever discussed it, and it is running underneath every hard week the two of you have.

This shows you which one you run, what your partner is left holding because of it, and the one sentence that would change how the next hard week goes.

What this is built on The four styles follow the Romantic Support-Seeking scale, which separates asking directly for practical help, asking directly for comfort, signalling without asking, and preferring to handle it alone. The good-news section follows Shelly Gable's work on capitalization, where the response to a partner's good news predicts relationship satisfaction more strongly than the response to bad news. The pursue-and-withdraw pattern is Christensen and Heavey's, whose work found it is driven by which partner wants the change, not by which one is the man.
