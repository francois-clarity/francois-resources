How to use it
01 · Notice

Catch the signal

Pause and check your body first. Tight jaw, heavy chest, buzzing stomach. Your body usually knows before your mind has words for it.

02 · Name

Find the exact word

Start with the family in the centre, then move outward until a word lands. "Frustrated" and "resentful" are cousins, but they ask for very different things.

03 · Listen

Hear what it wants

Every emotion carries a message. Anger defends a line. Sadness honours a loss. Fear asks for safety. Once you hear the message, you can choose your response.

When it feels too big

Sometimes a feeling is too loud to name from inside it. That is not failure, it is physiology. These three practices turn the volume down far enough for words to reach you again.

Practice one

The double breath

Two short breaths in through the nose, then one long, slow breath out through the mouth. Repeat for a minute, or five. In a Stanford trial, five minutes a day of this sigh-led breathing lifted mood and calmed the body more than meditation did.

Practice two

Say the word out loud

Literally. "I feel furious." Brain imaging shows that putting a feeling into words quietens the amygdala, the brain's alarm centre. Naming is not wallowing. It is how the alarm learns that someone is at the controls.

Practice three

Let the body finish it

Find where the feeling sits, chest, jaw, stomach, and rest your attention there without the story. Feelings move like waves. If you stay with the sensation instead of the commentary, the wave usually passes sooner than you expect.

Why naming matters

Naming an emotion changes it. The moment you move from "I feel bad" to "I feel resentful", the feeling loses some of its grip and gains a shape you can work with. Psychologists call this emotional granularity. I call it turning the lights on.

Feelings are information, not instructions. They tell you what matters, what hurts, what is missing and what needs protecting. You get to hear the message without obeying it blindly. That gap, between feeling something and acting on it, is where all your power lives.

So use this wheel slowly. Once a day is plenty. Ask yourself what you are feeling, find the word, save the check-in, and let the pattern teach you something over the weeks. Where attention goes, energy flows.

Naming calms the brain

In brain imaging at UCLA, putting a feeling into words lowered activity in the amygdala, the brain's alarm system, and engaged the reasoning cortex instead.

Lieberman and colleagues, 2007

Precision is a skill

People who can tell frustrated from resentful from disappointed regulate their emotions better and act on them less impulsively. The skill grows with practice, like any vocabulary.

Barrett and colleagues, on emotional granularity

Feelings live in the body

When more than 700 people mapped where they feel emotions, each basic emotion showed its own consistent pattern in the body. Your body is data, not noise.

Nummenmaa and colleagues, 2014

Five minutes is enough

In a Stanford study, five minutes a day of slow, sigh-led breathing improved mood and reduced anxiety within a month, outperforming mindfulness meditation.

Balban and colleagues, 2023
