Nobody is going to tell you whether you are an addict.

Not because it would be unkind, but because the honest answer is that the word does not do what people think it does. The World Health Organisation does list a compulsive sexual behaviour disorder, and classifies it as an impulse control problem rather than an addiction. The American manual looked at the same evidence and left it out altogether. There is no settled test to give you.

What there is, is a finding worth more than a label. When researchers measured what actually predicts somebody calling themselves a porn addict, the strongest predictor was not how much they used. It was how strongly they believed it was wrong. Two men doing exactly the same thing, one of whom thinks it is a sin, describe completely different levels of compulsion.

So this measures two things and it keeps them apart: what it is actually costing you, and how far it sits from what you believe. Both are real. They are not the same problem, and treating one as the other is how people spend years getting nowhere.

One last thing. Whatever the two numbers above say, you are not the worst thing you do in private, and the size of the gap between your behaviour and your beliefs is not a measure of your worth. It is a measure of a distance. Distances can be closed, and there is more than one direction to close them from.

What this is built on Joshua Grubbs and colleagues on moral incongruence, whose work found that self-reported addiction to pornography is predicted more strongly by moral disapproval of one's own use than by how much a person actually uses, and that the gap between belief and behaviour is itself a direct source of distress. The classification note is the ICD-11, which lists Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder as an impulse control disorder rather than an addiction, and the DSM-5, which declined to include hypersexual disorder on the grounds that the evidence did not show a distinct syndrome.
