Invisible Contracts

You agreed to something. Nobody said it out loud.

There are deals running in your relationships that were never negotiated. You keep your side. You expect the other side to be kept too. And when it is not, you feel it before you can explain it.

That flare is the doorway. We are going to follow it to one contract, get it onto the page in your words, and turn it into something you can actually say.

If you do that, I feel like this. If you do not do that, I feel like this. That is a contract.

Why you could not see it

You learned this before you had language. Small children work out how to be with the people they depend on, and that learning is stored as something you do rather than something you can recall. Researchers call it implicit relational knowing. It sits in action and feeling, never in words, which is why the contract cannot be remembered. It can only be performed.

Your brain then runs it as a prediction about what should happen next. When somebody breaks it, an error signal fires before any thinking starts. You feel the flare first and reach for the reason afterwards. That is why resentment is the doorway, and why the reason you give in the moment is usually about the dishes.

Ivan Boszormenyi-Nagy called the family version invisible loyalties: an unspoken account of what is owed and what is due, carried down the generations. Nobody reads the ledger out. Everybody keeps to it.

The contract had power while it stayed unnamed. It is on the page now, in your own words, and that is already a different position to stand in.
